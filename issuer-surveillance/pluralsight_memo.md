# Pluralsight — issuer memo

## One-line thesis
Pluralsight is the most instructive **completed software private-credit restructuring** in this universe: a large recurring-revenue take-private that went from ARR-based underwriting to lender control, debt-for-equity exchange, and PIK accruals — providing the clearest public template for how software credit stress migrates from mark-down to workout.

## What the company does
Pluralsight is a technology learning and skills-development platform used by enterprises and individuals for technical training. It once looked like a lender-friendly recurring-revenue software and content business: subscriptions, enterprise contracts, low churn optics, and a workforce-development narrative that seemed resilient across cycles.

The case is important precisely because that profile did not prevent a weak recovery and lender-control outcome. Recurring revenue, sponsor ownership, and mission-critical language were not sufficient to protect lenders when leverage was too high and interest burden rose faster than operating improvement.

## Why it matters
- **confirmed restructuring with public lender-group visibility**: Blue Owl, Ares, Golub, Oaktree, Benefit Street, Goldman Sachs, and BlackRock are among the disclosed lender/new-owner group
- **filing-confirmed post-restructuring exposure** at ARCC (PIK debt) and GSBD (debt + equity) in public BDC schedules
- **KBRA flagged it as a BDC bellwether**, noting exposure across rated BDC vehicles — validating this as a direct BDC-bond risk case
- the Pluralsight workout pattern — IP transfer, liability management, debt-for-equity — has become a template for how software private-credit stress plays out in practice
- **Recovery quality was poor**: equity marked to zero by Vista before the workout; lenders became equity holders in a company under operational stress

## What happened — the case study

### Phase 1: Underwriting and close (2021)
Vista took Pluralsight private using over $1.5 billion in debt, including a $1.175 billion recurring-revenue loan. The underwriting was predicated on ARR stability, enterprise retention, and software learning platform defensibility. At close, the leverage looked manageable under base-case assumptions.

### Phase 2: Deterioration (2022–2023)
Several dynamics converged:
- interest burden increased faster than operating improvement, particularly as rates rose
- growth slowed materially; the workforce-development thesis faced pressure from free alternatives and shifting enterprise learning budgets
- equity was effectively written to zero by Vista before any formal restructuring

### Phase 3: Liability management (2024)
A sponsor-supported LME involved moving IP to an unrestricted subsidiary, creating a new structurally senior layer for lender negotiations. This type of move — common in private-credit workouts — reflects the reality that in asset-light software credits, **IP is often the only meaningful collateral**, and lenders who control the IP negotiation have a stronger hand in restructuring.

### Phase 4: Restructuring close (2024)
Lenders including Blue Owl, Ares, Golub, Oaktree, Benefit Street, Goldman Sachs, and BlackRock take ownership through a debt-for-equity exchange. The reorganized company carries a post-restructuring debt stack with PIK features (reflecting the need to preserve cash for operations) and new equity held by the former lenders.

## Why it is the best historical teaching case

| Lesson | What Pluralsight shows |
|--------|----------------------|
| ARR underwriting ≠ downside protection | Recurring revenue did not prevent a 0-recovery equity and weak lender outcome |
| Sponsor optionality can work against lenders | Vista moved IP before lenders could stabilize the situation |
| Rate sensitivity matters | Unhedged floating-rate debt became unserviceable as SOFR rose |
| Software recovery = franchise value, not assets | Hard collateral was near zero; recovery depended on preserving the platform |
| PIK is a workout artifact | Post-restructuring PIK debt is not strength — it reflects inability to service cash-pay terms |
| Lender-as-equity is not the same as winning | Holding reorganized equity at $13M against a multi-hundred-million pre-workout cost is still a large loss |

## What to look for in similar names
- ARR-based underwriting without clear EBITDA or cash interest coverage
- sponsor actions that move assets before distress is public (IP transfers, unrestricted subsidiary moves)
- unhedged floating-rate debt at high leverage
- software platform with meaningful competitor displacement risk
- lender coordination signals early in the deterioration cycle

## BDC bond relevance
The Pluralsight pattern is directly relevant to BDC unsecured bond investors because:
1. KBRA explicitly flagged it as a bellwether for BDC credit exposure
2. the lender group included platforms (Ares, Blue Owl, Goldman, Golub) that manage prominent BDCs
3. post-restructuring PIK debt at ARCC and equity at GSBD are visible in filed schedules — so the mark impact is already partially flowing through public BDC disclosures
4. the workout logic, IP-transfer playbook, and recovery magnitude provide a concrete baseline for how similar software credits might resolve
